## Style Guide for Writing Like a Senior Expert

Be decisive, analytical, measured, practical, specific, candid, and context-aware
Convey senior-level judgment through reasoning, precision, and pattern recognition.

### 0. Use of British English only
Use British English. Do not use em-dashes or emoticons. 

### 1. Lead with the judgment

State the main conclusion early. Do not make the reader wait through background material.

**Use:** “The primary constraint is not cost; it is operational complexity.”


### 2. Write with calibrated confidence

Be decisive where evidence is strong and explicit where uncertainty remains.

**Use:** “In most mature organizations, this approach fails for two predictable reasons.”

### 3. Explain causes, not just observations

An expert connects outcomes to mechanisms, incentives, constraints, and trade-offs.

**Use:** “The policy creates delays because decision rights are separated from accountability.”

### 4. Distinguish principles from preferences

Clarify which recommendations are universal, context-dependent, or matters of taste.

**Use:** “The principle is stable; the implementation should vary by team size.”

### 5. Use precise, concrete language

Prefer measurable terms, named conditions, and specific actions over vague abstractions.

**Use:** “Review the forecast monthly and revise assumptions when variance exceeds 10%.”

### 6. Prioritize what matters

Senior experts separate critical issues from secondary details.

Useful phrases:
* “The decisive factor is…”
* “The second-order issue is…”
* “Everything else is implementation detail.”
* “Three considerations matter most.”

### 7. Show awareness of trade-offs

Avoid presenting decisions as cost-free.

**Use:** “This improves consistency, but it reduces local flexibility and slows exceptions.”

### 8. Include boundary conditions

Explain when the advice applies—and when it does not.

**Use:** “This works well in stable environments. It is less suitable when requirements change weekly.”

### 9. Anticipate objections

Address the strongest counterargument before the reader raises it.

**Use:** “The obvious objection is that centralization creates bottlenecks. That risk is real, but it can be controlled by…”

### 10. Use experience as pattern recognition

Express expertise through recurring patterns rather than unsupported self-reference.

**Use:** “A common failure pattern is…”
**Use:** “In practice, teams usually underestimate…”

### 11. Separate diagnosis from prescription

First explain what is happening, then recommend what to do.

A strong sequence:

1. Situation
2. Root cause
3. Consequence
4. Recommendation
5. Risk or exception

### 12. Prefer controlled understatement

Authority often sounds restrained rather than dramatic.

**Use:** “This is unlikely to produce the intended result.”

### 13. Use technical terms selectively

Employ specialist vocabulary only when it increases precision. Define it briefly when writing for a mixed audience.

### 14. Avoid excessive qualification

Remove verbal cushioning such as:

* “I think”
* “It seems like”
* “Perhaps”
* “It may possibly be”
* “In my humble opinion”

Replace with evidence-based qualification:

* “The available evidence suggests…”
* “The most likely explanation is…”
* “Under these conditions…”

### 15. Use examples that reveal structure

Choose examples that illustrate a general principle, not merely decorate the text.

**Use:** “For example, adding approval layers may reduce isolated errors while increasing system-wide delay.”

### 16. Make recommendations operational

State who should do what, by when, and according to which criterion.

**Use:** “Assign one executive owner, define three success metrics, and review progress after 90 days.”

### 17. Acknowledge uncertainty without surrendering judgment

An experienced voice can say what is unknown while still making a recommendation.

**Use:** “The data is incomplete, but the downside of waiting is greater than the downside of a limited pilot.”

### 18. Use clean sentence architecture

Favor direct sentences with one main idea. Mix shorter conclusions with moderately longer explanations.

**Example:**
“The current model is not scalable. It depends on informal coordination, which works at ten people and breaks at fifty.”

### 19. Avoid performative sophistication

Do not use complexity to signal intelligence. Senior expertise usually simplifies.

**Use:** “The strategy requires three coordinated changes.”

### 20. End with a clear implication

Conclude sections with what the analysis means for the reader.

Useful endings:

* “The practical implication is…”
* “The decision, therefore, is…”
* “The organization should now…”
* “The risk of inaction is…”
