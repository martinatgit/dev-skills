# Style Guide for Reviewing a Senior Expert Voice

Use this guide to review whether a text conveys credible, senior-level authority.

The target voice is:

**Decisive, analytical, measured, practical, specific, candid, and context-aware.**

Authority should come from reasoning, precision, prioritisation, and pattern recognition. It should not come from status claims, inflated certainty, theatrical language, or unnecessary complexity.

## Reviewer Instructions

For each issue identified:

1. Quote the relevant wording.
2. Name the violated principle.
3. Explain briefly why the wording weakens the senior expert voice.
4. Suggest a specific revision.
5. Classify the issue:

   * **Major:** materially weakens credibility, clarity, or judgment.
   * **Moderate:** noticeable stylistic weakness.
   * **Minor:** local wording or consistency issue.

Do not flag a passage merely because it is formal, technical, cautious, or concise. Flag it only when the wording conflicts with the principles below.

Do not rewrite the entire text unless requested. Prioritise the changes that would most improve authority, clarity, and usefulness.

## General Antipatterns

Flag language that is:

* Boastful
* Theatrical
* Absolute without sufficient evidence
* Vague
* Condescending
* Overly academic
* Needlessly complicated
* Repetitive
* Formulaic
* Unsupported by reasoning
* Detached from practical implications

Flag direct or indirect claims of authority based on credentials, seniority, reputation, or years of experience.

Examples to flag:

* “As a recognised industry leader...”
* “With more than 20 years of experience...”
* “As any experienced executive knows...”
* “Trust me on this.”
* “I have seen this countless times.”

Expertise should be demonstrated through the quality of the analysis, not asserted through biography or status.

---

## 0. Use British English

Use standard British-English spelling and usage consistently.

Flag:

* American-English spellings, such as “organization”, “prioritize”, “analyze”, “behavior”, and “center”
* Inconsistent mixing of British and American spelling
* American usage where a clear British equivalent is expected
* Em dashes
* Emoticons and decorative symbols
* Unexplained foreign-language characters in otherwise English prose

Do not flag:

* Proper names
* Registered product or company names
* Direct quotations that must preserve the original spelling
* Foreign terms that are necessary, correctly used, and explained
* Code, identifiers, URLs, or technical strings

Prefer:

* “organisation”
* “prioritise”
* “analyse”
* “behaviour”
* “centre”

Replace em dashes with commas, colons, or separate sentences.

## 1. Lead with the judgment

State the main conclusion early. Do not make the reader wait through background, scene-setting, or a list of considerations.

**Use:**
“The primary constraint is not cost; it is operational complexity.”

**Avoid:**
“There are several factors that may be worth considering.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Long introductions before the conclusion
* Opening with generic context
* Delaying the recommendation until the final paragraph
* Beginning with a list of factors without identifying which one matters most
* Using rhetorical questions instead of stating the judgment
* Hiding the conclusion behind phrases such as “It is worth noting that”

---

## 2. Write with calibrated confidence

Be decisive where the evidence is strong. State uncertainty explicitly where evidence is incomplete.

**Use:**
“In most mature organisations, this approach fails for two predictable reasons.”

**Avoid:**
“This will definitely work in every situation.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Absolute claims such as “always”, “never”, “guaranteed”, or “will certainly”
* Confidence that exceeds the evidence
* Excessive caution that prevents a usable conclusion
* Treating assumptions as established facts
* False precision
* Repeated use of “may”, “might”, or “could” without explaining the source of uncertainty

---

## 3. Explain causes, not just observations

Connect outcomes to mechanisms, incentives, constraints, dependencies, and trade-offs.

**Use:**
“The policy creates delays because decision rights are separated from accountability.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Describing a problem without explaining why it occurs
* Listing symptoms as though they were root causes
* Using labels such as “culture problem” or “execution issue” without analysis
* Presenting correlation as causation
* Making recommendations that are not connected to the diagnosis
* Relying on phrases such as “This is common” without explaining the underlying pattern

---

## 4. Distinguish principles from preferences

Clarify whether a recommendation is broadly applicable, context-dependent, or a matter of taste.

**Use:**
“The principle is stable; the implementation should vary by team size.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Presenting a personal preference as a universal rule
* Treating one implementation method as the only valid option
* Failing to distinguish required controls from optional practices
* Using “best practice” without specifying the relevant context
* Rejecting alternatives without explaining the governing principle
* Confusing stylistic consistency with operational necessity

---

## 5. Use precise, concrete language

Prefer measurable terms, named conditions, and specific actions over vague abstractions.

**Use:**
“Review the forecast monthly and revise assumptions when variance exceeds 10%.”

**Avoid:**
“Monitor performance regularly.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Vague frequency terms such as “regularly”, “often”, or “periodically”
* Unspecified actors, such as “the business” or “stakeholders”
* Abstract verbs such as “optimise”, “enhance”, or “drive” without a defined outcome
* Ambiguous pronouns
* Undefined success criteria
* Recommendations that cannot be acted upon or verified

---

## 6. Prioritise what matters

Separate decisive issues from secondary considerations.

Useful phrases include:

* “The decisive factor is...”
* “The second-order issue is...”
* “Everything else is implementation detail.”
* “Three considerations matter most.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Giving every issue equal weight
* Long undifferentiated lists
* Focusing on minor wording or process details before strategic risks
* Failing to identify the main constraint
* Presenting more than five priorities without ranking them
* Treating an interesting observation as a decision-relevant issue
* Burying the material risk among minor concerns

---

## 7. Show awareness of trade-offs

Do not present decisions as cost-free. Identify what improves, what deteriorates, and who bears the cost.

**Use:**
“This improves consistency, but it reduces local flexibility and slows exceptions.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* One-sided advocacy
* Claims of “win-win” outcomes without evidence
* Recommendations that mention benefits but omit costs
* Treating efficiency, quality, speed, control, and flexibility as simultaneously maximisable
* Ignoring implementation burden
* Failing to identify who gains and who loses
* Describing a compromise as an unqualified improvement

---

## 8. Include boundary conditions

Explain when the advice applies and when it does not.

**Use:**
“This works well in stable environments. It is less suitable when requirements change weekly.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Universal recommendations based on a narrow context
* Failure to specify scale, maturity, risk level, or operating environment
* Advice that assumes resources, authority, or data are available
* No discussion of exceptions
* Ignoring material differences between small and large organisations
* Using a single case as proof of general applicability

---

## 9. Anticipate objections

Address the strongest credible counterargument before the reader raises it.

**Use:**
“The obvious objection is that centralisation creates bottlenecks. That risk is real, but it can be controlled by delegating routine exceptions.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Ignoring an obvious objection
* Addressing only weak or implausible counterarguments
* Dismissing concerns as resistance, fear, or lack of understanding
* Using a straw-man argument
* Treating disagreement as evidence of incompetence
* Acknowledging an objection without answering it
* Claiming that critics “fail to understand” without engaging with their reasoning

---

## 10. Use experience as pattern recognition

Express expertise through recurring patterns, mechanisms, and predictable failure modes rather than unsupported self-reference.

**Use:**
“A common failure pattern is...”

**Use:**
“In practice, teams usually underestimate...”

**Avoid:**
“Trust me, I have seen everything.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Claims based on years of experience
* Appeals to personal authority
* References to seniority, credentials, clients, or reputation
* Anecdotes presented as conclusive evidence
* “I have seen this many times” without explaining the pattern
* Assertions that ask the reader to trust the writer rather than the reasoning

---

## 11. Separate diagnosis from prescription

Explain what is happening before recommending what should be done.

A strong sequence is:

1. Situation
2. Root cause
3. Consequence
4. Recommendation
5. Risk or exception

### Antipatterns to flag

* Jumping directly to a solution
* Recommending a tool before defining the problem
* Mixing diagnosis and prescription in a way that obscures both
* Treating the preferred solution as evidence for the diagnosis
* Providing actions without explaining the expected mechanism
* Repeating the problem statement as the recommendation
* Failing to explain the consequence of inaction

---

## 12. Prefer controlled understatement

Authority usually sounds restrained rather than dramatic.

**Use:**
“This is unlikely to produce the intended result.”

**Avoid:**
“This strategy is a complete disaster.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Catastrophic language
* Exaggerated praise or criticism
* Sensational adjectives
* Mockery or sarcasm
* Emotional intensifiers such as “incredibly”, “utterly”, or “obviously”
* Claims that a routine issue is “transformational”, “revolutionary”, or “existential”
* Dramatic metaphors that replace analysis

---

## 13. Use technical terms selectively

Use specialist vocabulary only when it increases precision. Define unfamiliar terms when writing for a mixed audience.

### Antipatterns to flag

* Jargon that could be replaced by plain language
* Acronyms that are not defined on first use
* Technical vocabulary used to signal status rather than meaning
* Dense clusters of abstract nouns
* Terms borrowed from another discipline without a clear purpose
* Unnecessary Latin expressions
* Assuming specialist knowledge that the intended audience may not possess

Do not flag a technical term merely because it is specialised. Flag it when it is unnecessary, undefined, or less precise than a simpler alternative.

---

## 14. Avoid excessive qualification

Remove verbal cushioning that weakens otherwise supportable judgments.

Examples to review:

* “I think”
* “It seems like”
* “Perhaps”
* “It may possibly be”
* “In my humble opinion”

Replace them with evidence-based qualification:

* “The available evidence suggests...”
* “The most likely explanation is...”
* “Under these conditions...”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Multiple qualifiers in the same sentence
* Personal disclaimers before a recommendation
* Hedging used to avoid responsibility for a conclusion
* “Possibly”, “perhaps”, and “might” used without identifying uncertainty
* Apologetic framing
* Statements so qualified that they no longer guide a decision

Do not remove qualification when uncertainty is material. Replace vague hesitation with a specific statement about evidence, assumptions, probability, or conditions.

---

## 15. Use examples that reveal structure

Choose examples that demonstrate a general mechanism or decision rule.

**Use:**
“For example, adding approval layers may reduce isolated errors while increasing system-wide delay.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Decorative anecdotes that do not support the argument
* Examples that are more complex than the principle
* Extreme or implausible scenarios
* Examples that introduce unrelated issues
* A single anecdote presented as proof
* Examples with no explanation of what they demonstrate
* Name-dropping organisations or individuals without analytical value

---

## 16. Make recommendations operational

State who should act, what they should do, by when, and according to which criterion.

**Use:**
“Assign one executive owner, define three success metrics, and review progress after 90 days.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Generic recommendations such as “improve communication”
* No named owner
* No timing, sequence, or trigger
* No completion or success criterion
* Actions outside the recipient’s authority
* Recommendations that depend on unspecified future analysis
* Advice that cannot be converted into a decision or next step
* Lists of activities with no indication of priority

Not every recommendation requires a calendar date. It should, however, include enough detail to guide execution.

---

## 17. Acknowledge uncertainty without surrendering judgment

State what is unknown, explain why it matters, and still provide a reasoned recommendation.

**Use:**
“The data is incomplete, but the downside of waiting is greater than the downside of a limited pilot.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Refusing to recommend because information is incomplete
* Pretending uncertainty does not exist
* Listing unknowns without assessing their decision relevance
* Using uncertainty as a reason for indefinite delay
* Recommending more research without defining what decision it would change
* Treating all uncertain outcomes as equally likely
* Presenting speculation as fact

---

## 18. Use clean sentence architecture

Prefer direct sentences with one main idea. Combine short conclusions with moderately longer explanations.

**Use:**
“The current model is not scalable. It depends on informal coordination, which works at ten people and breaks at fifty.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Sentences with several competing ideas
* Long chains of subordinate clauses
* Excessive parenthetical remarks
* Unclear pronoun references
* Repeated nominalisations, such as “the implementation of the optimisation of”
* Passive voice that obscures responsibility
* Multiple qualifications inserted before the main verb
* Paragraphs with no clear controlling idea

Do not flag sentence length alone. Flag sentences that are difficult to parse, conceal the main point, or combine ideas that should be separated.

---

## 19. Avoid performative sophistication

Do not use complexity to signal intelligence. Senior expertise usually simplifies the issue without distorting it.

**Avoid:**
“The multidimensional strategic paradigm necessitates...”

**Use:**
“The strategy requires three coordinated changes.”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Inflated abstract language
* Unnecessary conceptual frameworks
* Long words used where short ones are more precise
* Excessive use of nouns instead of verbs
* Invented labels for familiar ideas
* Dense prose that requires interpretation before it can be acted upon
* References to “paradigms”, “ecosystems”, “synergies”, or “transformation” without specific meaning
* Complexity that does not improve accuracy

---

## 20. End with a clear implication

Conclude each substantive section with what the analysis means for the reader, decision, or next action.

Useful endings include:

* “The practical implication is...”
* “The decision, therefore, is...”
* “The organisation should now...”
* “The risk of inaction is...”

### Antipatterns to flag

* Ending with background information
* Introducing a new issue in the final sentence
* Repeating the analysis without stating its significance
* Ending with a rhetorical question
* Closing with “more research is needed” without defining the next decision
* Ending on a vague aspiration
* Failing to identify the decision, action, risk, or consequence

---

# Recommended Voice Profile

The text should sound:

* Decisive, but not absolute
* Analytical, but not academic
* Measured, but not hesitant
* Practical, but not simplistic
* Specific, but not overloaded with detail
* Candid, but not abrasive
* Context-aware, but not paralysed by exceptions
* Authoritative, but not self-important

## Voice Antipatterns

Flag a voice that sounds:

* Boastful
* Self-congratulatory
* Theatrical
* Prophetic
* Condescending
* Dismissive
* Defensive
* Overconfident
* Excessively cautious
* Bureaucratic
* Academic for its own sake
* Artificially polished
* Formulaic or repetitive
* More concerned with sounding intelligent than helping the reader decide

# Ethical Principle

Convey senior-level judgment through reasoning, precision, prioritisation, and pattern recognition rather than through claims of credentials, status, reputation, privileged access, or years of experience.

Do not manufacture authority by implying personal experience that has not been established. Do not present invented examples, observations, clients, projects, or professional history as real.

Where authority depends on evidence, make the evidence or reasoning visible. Where uncertainty remains, state it accurately. The objective is credible judgment, not the performance of expertise.

# Final Review Check

Before completing the review, determine whether the text:

1. States its main judgment early.
2. Explains the mechanism behind its conclusions.
3. Prioritises the issues that matter most.
4. Uses confidence proportionate to the evidence.
5. Identifies trade-offs and boundary conditions.
6. Converts analysis into operational recommendations.
7. Uses clear British English.
8. Avoids self-credentialing and performative authority.
9. Ends with a clear implication.
10. Helps the reader make a better decision.

The final review should focus on the few changes that would most improve credibility and usefulness. Do not overwhelm the writer with minor stylistic comments while leaving structural weaknesses unaddressed.
