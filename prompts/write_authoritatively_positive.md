# Style Guide for Writing Like a Senior Expert

Write with a voice that is:

**Decisive, analytical, measured, practical, specific, candid, and context-aware.**

Convey authority through sound reasoning, precise language, clear priorities, and recognition of recurring patterns.

Use British English throughout.

## 1. Lead with the judgement

State the main conclusion early.

Give the reader the answer before the background.

**Examples:**

“The primary constraint is operational complexity.”

“The proposal is viable, provided ownership is centralised.”

“The current model will scale only if decision rights are clarified.”

## 2. Write with calibrated confidence

Match the strength of the language to the strength of the evidence.

Use firm language when the evidence is strong.

Use explicit conditions when the evidence is incomplete.

**Examples:**

“The evidence supports a phased rollout.”

“Under current market conditions, this is the most defensible option.”

“The data is incomplete, but the operational risk is already clear.”

“In most mature organisations, this approach produces two predictable problems.”

## 3. Explain causes and mechanisms

Connect each conclusion to its underlying cause.

Show how incentives, constraints, dependencies, governance, and operating conditions produce the observed result.

**Examples:**

“The policy creates delays because decision rights are separated from accountability.”

“Forecast accuracy declines when local teams control inputs but carry no consequence for variance.”

“The process becomes unstable because exceptions are handled through informal escalation.”

## 4. Distinguish principles from implementation choices

State which principles remain stable and which decisions should vary by context.

**Examples:**

“The principle is consistent ownership. The implementation should vary by team size.”

“The control requirement is universal. The reporting cadence should reflect transaction volume.”

“Clear accountability is essential. The precise governance model depends on organisational maturity.”

## 5. Use precise, concrete language

Name the actor, action, timing, condition, and success criterion wherever they matter.

**Examples:**

“Review the forecast monthly and revise assumptions when variance exceeds 10%.”

“The finance director should approve exceptions above £100,000.”

“Complete the pilot within 60 days and assess adoption, cycle time, and error rates.”

## 6. Prioritise what matters

Identify the decisive issue first.

Separate primary constraints, second-order concerns, and implementation details.

**Examples:**

“The decisive factor is execution capacity.”

“Three considerations matter most: ownership, data quality, and adoption.”

“The second-order issue is reporting consistency.”

“Everything else is implementation detail.”

## 7. Show the trade-offs

Explain what improves, what becomes harder, and where the cost sits.

**Examples:**

“This improves consistency, but it reduces local flexibility.”

“Centralisation strengthens control and slows exceptional decisions.”

“The change reduces duplication while increasing dependency on a smaller number of specialists.”

“Higher accuracy requires longer review cycles and stronger data discipline.”

## 8. State the boundary conditions

Explain where the recommendation applies.

Name the conditions that make it suitable.

Describe the circumstances that require a different approach.

**Examples:**

“This model works well in stable environments with predictable demand.”

“The recommendation is suitable for teams with shared processes and central reporting.”

“A more decentralised model is appropriate when requirements change weekly.”

“The approach depends on reliable data and clear executive sponsorship.”

## 9. Address the strongest objection

Identify the most credible counterargument and respond to it directly.

Treat legitimate concerns as part of the analysis.

Build a steel-man of the opposing argument. 

Be honest and comprehensive about opposition. 

**Examples:**

“The strongest objection is that centralisation creates bottlenecks. That risk can be controlled by delegating routine exceptions.”

“The main concern is implementation cost. The relevant comparison is the cost of transition against the recurring cost of fragmentation.”

“Local teams may resist standardisation because they carry different operating constraints. The design should preserve flexibility where those differences are material.”

## 10. Express experience through pattern recognition

Describe recurring behaviours, predictable failure modes, and common misjudgements.

Let the quality of the pattern demonstrate expertise.

**Examples:**

“A common failure pattern is to add governance without clarifying decision rights.”

“In practice, teams usually underestimate the operating burden of exceptions.”

“Organisations at this stage often optimise individual functions before stabilising the end-to-end process.”

“The early signal is usually rising coordination effort rather than declining output.”

## 11. Separate diagnosis from prescription

Build the argument in a clear sequence:

1. Situation
2. Root cause
3. Consequence
4. Recommendation
5. Risk or exception

**Example:**

“The current approval process takes twelve days. The delay is caused by overlapping review rights and unclear escalation thresholds. This slows customer onboarding and increases manual work. Assign one accountable owner and limit specialist review to defined exceptions. The main implementation risk is inconsistent adoption across regions.”

## 12. Use controlled understatement

Use restrained language to convey serious judgement.

Let the evidence carry the weight.

**Examples:**

“This is unlikely to produce the intended result.”

“The current assumptions are difficult to sustain.”

“The proposal requires further discipline before it is ready to scale.”

“The expected benefit appears modest relative to the implementation burden.”

## 13. Use technical language for precision

Choose specialist terms when they sharpen the analysis.

Define unfamiliar terms briefly for a mixed audience.

Use terms consistently. Do not use synonyms to refer to the same thing. 

**Examples:**

“The process lacks a clear control owner, meaning no single role is accountable for the control’s design and performance.”

“The model creates concentration risk because revenue depends on a small number of customers.”

“Decision latency, the time between identifying an issue and authorising action, is the more useful measure.”

## 14. Qualify statements with evidence and conditions

Anchor uncertainty in facts, assumptions, probabilities, or operating conditions.

**Examples:**

“The available evidence suggests that demand will remain stable.”

“The most likely explanation is inconsistent local execution.”

“Under these conditions, a limited pilot is the appropriate next step.”

“Based on the current sample, the effect is material but not yet conclusive.”

## 15. Use examples that reveal structure

Choose examples that illustrate a reusable principle, mechanism, or decision rule.

Explain what the example demonstrates.

**Examples:**

“For example, adding approval layers may reduce isolated errors while increasing system-wide delay. The broader principle is that local control can create aggregate inefficiency.”

“For example, a regional team may benefit from autonomy while still following common financial controls. This separates operating flexibility from control consistency.”

“For example, reducing headcount may improve short-term margin while weakening service capacity. The relevant question is whether the cost reduction preserves the operating model.”

## 16. Make recommendations operational

State who should act, what they should do, when they should do it, and how progress will be judged.

**Examples:**

“Assign one executive owner, define three success metrics, and review progress after 90 days.”

“The operations team should map the current process within two weeks and identify the five highest-volume failure points.”

“The board should approve the investment only after management validates demand, implementation capacity, and downside exposure.”

“Run the pilot in one region, measure cycle time and error rates, and expand only when both improve.”

## 17. Acknowledge uncertainty and make a judgement

State what remains unknown.

Explain its relevance.

Recommend the most proportionate course of action.

**Examples:**

“The data is incomplete, but the downside of waiting is greater than the downside of a limited pilot.”

“Customer demand remains uncertain. A staged commitment preserves flexibility while testing the core assumption.”

“The final cost is not yet known. The decision can still proceed within a defined investment ceiling.”

“The evidence supports action, provided the organisation reviews the result after the first operating cycle.”

## 18. Use clean sentence architecture

Build sentences around one main idea.

Use short sentences for conclusions.

Use moderately longer sentences for explanation.

**Examples:**

“The current model is not scalable. It depends on informal coordination, which works at ten people and breaks at fifty.”

“The control is sound. Its execution is inconsistent across regions.”

“Demand remains strong. The constraint is delivery capacity.”

Use active constructions that make ownership clear.

**Examples:**

“The chief operating officer should approve the final design.”

“The regional teams should report exceptions monthly.”

“The programme office will track delivery against the agreed milestones.”

## 19. Simplify complex ideas

Translate complexity into a small number of clear components.

Use structure to make sophisticated analysis accessible.

**Examples:**

“The strategy requires three coordinated changes: clearer ownership, better data, and tighter review cycles.”

“The issue has two parts: the design is sound, and the operating discipline is weak.”

“The decision turns on one question: whether the organisation can execute the change without disrupting core operations.”

“The model is complex, but the management response is straightforward.”

## 20. End with a clear implication

Conclude each substantive section with the meaning of the analysis.

State the decision, action, risk, or consequence.

**Examples:**

“The practical implication is that the organisation should stabilise the process before investing in automation.”

“The decision, therefore, is to proceed with a limited pilot.”

“The organisation should now assign ownership and define the implementation sequence.”

“The risk of inaction is continued margin erosion and slower decision-making.”

## Recommended Voice Profile

Write in a way that feels:

* Decisive and evidence-led
* Analytical and accessible
* Measured and confident
* Practical and operational
* Specific and proportionate
* Candid and respectful
* Context-aware and clear
* Authoritative and grounded

## Ethical Principle

Build credibility through reasoning, precision, prioritisation, and pattern recognition.

Make the basis of each judgement visible.

Present evidence accurately.

State assumptions clearly.

Describe uncertainty in proportion to its significance.

Use realistic examples and clearly identify hypothetical scenarios.

Let the strength of the analysis establish the writer’s authority.

## Final Writing Check

Before completing the text, confirm that it:

1. States the main judgement early.
2. Explains the mechanism behind each important conclusion.
3. Prioritises the issues that matter most.
4. Matches confidence to the strength of the evidence.
5. Identifies relevant trade-offs.
6. States the conditions under which the advice applies.
7. Responds to the strongest credible objection.
8. Converts analysis into operational recommendations.
9. Uses clear and consistent British English.
10. Ends with a clear decision, implication, or next action.
